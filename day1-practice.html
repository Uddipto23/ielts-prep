The Return of the Urban Tram

A Trams dominated city transport across Europe and North America in the early twentieth century. By the 1960s, however, most British and American cities had torn up their networks entirely. Planners of the period were convinced that buses were cheaper to operate and far more flexible, since a bus route can be altered overnight while a tram route cannot.

B The reversal began on the continent. From the 1980s onwards several French and German cities reintroduced trams, often as the centrepiece of wider schemes to pedestrianise city centres. British cities followed more slowly and less consistently.

C The arguments in favour are well established. A single tram can carry several times the passengers of a bus, it runs on electricity rather than diesel, and its low floor allows step-free boarding for wheelchair users and parents with pushchairs. Contrary to the common assumption that construction cost is the decisive obstacle, the greater difficulty is usually disruption: laying track closes streets for months at a time, and nearby shops frequently lose trade during the works.

D Opponents are not so much arguing that trams fail to move people efficiently as that they lock a city into a fixed route for decades. Should the pattern of a city shift, a tram line cannot follow it. Supporters treat that same permanence as an advantage, pointing out that developers invest near infrastructure they believe will still be there in thirty years.

E The results have been mixed. Some systems carry far more passengers than forecast, while others have fallen well short of their projections. Analysts initially predicted that the most recent generation of schemes would be self-financing within a decade. In practice, almost all continue to require public subsidy.

Part C — The rest of Day 1

Reading is one skill of four. These take about 50 minutes together.

Writing · 40 minutes

  • Prompt: Some people believe university education should be free for all students, while others argue students should pay for it themselves. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.
  • Minimum 250 words. Type it — IELTS is computer-delivered now.
  • Check only five things: word count reached, four clear paragraphs, a stated position in the introduction, a topic sentence opening each body paragraph, and a conclusion that matches the introduction.
  • Do not grade the language yet. Structure first.

Speaking · 10 minutes, recorded

  • Part 1: Where are you from? Do you work or study? What do you do in your free time? Is your hometown a good place for young people?
  • Part 2 cue card, 1 minute preparation then 2 minutes speaking: Describe a skill you learned that was difficult at first. Say what it was, how you learned it, why it was difficult, and how you feel about it now.
  • Listen back and count three things: pauses longer than two seconds, filler words, and whether you reached two minutes.

Listening · 15 minutes

  • Use the free official sample questions on ielts.org, or Road to IELTS if you have registered with the British Council.
  • Do one Part 1 and one Part 4. Score them separately.
  • The gap between those two numbers tells you more than the total does.
